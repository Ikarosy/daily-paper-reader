# Abstract 

Neural audio codecs compress waveforms into compact discrete tokens that un-derpin speech language models, real-time communication, and large-scale audio storage. Almost every dominant design, including residual vector quantization, finite scalar quantization, and single-codebook variants, follows the VQ-VAE tem-plate by partitioning the encoder latent through a learned codebook or a fixed scalar grid. We ask whether this partition is necessary. We introduce GS-Codec, a neural speech codec whose bottleneck is a parametric signal decomposition rather than a quantizer. We adapt Gaussian splatting from 3D scene reconstruction to one-dimensional latents. An inner optimization loop fits each encoder segment as a weighted sum of 1D Gaussian primitives. The decoder then reconstructs the waveform from the rendered sum. To avoid the cost of this iterative inner loop at inference time, we additionally train a lightweight GS Predictor Net that regresses the primitive parameters in a single forward pass. The encoder and decoder are trained end-to-end through the inner loop, with no quantizer anywhere in the train-ing pipeline: the bottleneck is the decomposition itself, and scalar quantization is applied only post-training to the fitted parameters. Rather than relying on dis-crete codebook stages for bitrate control, our representation exposes a fine-grained rate-quality tradeoff: a single trained checkpoint supports post-training bitrate control by varying the number of primitives and the per-parameter bit depth, with no retraining required. GS-Codec matches or exceeds well-established open-source codecs such as EnCodec and DAC on speaker similarity (SIM), intelligibility (STOI), and perceptual quality (UTMOS) at comparable bitrates, while achieving comparable semantic performance (WER). Code and audio samples are available at https://ronaluf.github.io/gs-codec/ .

# 1 Introduction 

In the wake of Large Language Models [8, 84, 18, 78, 79, 61], there is growing demand for unified multimodal architectures and efficient audio compression. Neural audio codecs have emerged as foundational technology, compressing raw waveforms via neural networks [97, 16, 42] and tokenizing them into compact discrete representations that bridge high-rate acoustic signals and transformer-based models [6, 101]. These tokens are essential for TTS [85, 12, 47, 7, 35, 70, 32], music generation [1, 15, 6, 40, 46], voice conversion, dialogue [17, 101, 31, 20], and real-time communication [97, 96, 81]. Token fidelity directly bounds synthesis quality in LM-based TTS [85, 6] and music generation [1, 15], making codecs the critical infrastructure for multimodal audio systems [17, 100, 68]. The dominant architecture [16, 42, 93, 73] pairs a convolutional encoder with a Residual Vector Quantization (RVQ) bottleneck [97] and mirror decoder. RVQ iteratively quantizes residual error across codebook stages, enabling flexible bitrate-quality trade-offs; EnCodec [16] and DAC [42]

achieve near-transparent reconstruction at 3-6 kbps. However, these models share a structural assumption: compression must proceed through a learned discrete codebook, committing the latent to a lookup over a finite set of vectors fixed at training time. Bitrate adjusts only in discrete steps defined by the number of codebook stages, and the representation is susceptible to codebook collapse [19, 44, 106], wasting representational capacity. Whether the bottleneck can instead be a continuous parametric decomposition that encodes structure rather than assigns indices remains an open question. We introduce GS-Codec , a neural audio codec that replaces the RVQ quantizer with a Gaussian Splatting (GS) bottleneck. Building on Gaussian splatting’s success in image representation and compression [102, 52, 103, 99], GS-Codec fits NG localized Gaussian primitives to each audio segment’s latent via a lightweight per-clip optimizer. Each primitive shares a temporal width and per-channel amplitudes across a local neighbourhood of frames. This overlapping representation provides a structural prior at the bottleneck that framewise quantization does not explicitly impose. Rather than mapping to discrete codes (RVQ) or a fixed scalar grid (FSQ), GS-Codec uses a para-metric signal decomposition: the encoder latent is approximated as a weighted sum of Gaussian primitives, with only those parameters scalar-quantized post-training. Since quantization is applied after training, a single checkpoint supports fine-grained post-training bitrate control by varying the number of primitives and per-parameter bit depth, with no retraining required. Experiments on LibriTTS, LJSpeech, and LibriSpeech show GS-Codec matches or exceeds established open-source codecs on perceptual quality and speaker similarity, while opening new design axes for neural audio compression. Our contributions are as follows: 1. We propose GS-Codec, a neural speech codec whose bottleneck is a 1D Gaussian-splatting decomposition rather than a vector or scalar quantizer. To our knowledge, this is the first neural codec to replace partition-based discretization with a parametric signal decomposition inside the latent bottleneck. 2. We introduce a lightweight GS Predictor Net that learns to approximate the iterative Gaussian Splatting process, amortizing the multi-step optimization loop into a single forward pass for fast inference. 3. We demonstrate fine-grained post-training bitrate control via varying primitive count and bit depth, and report comparisons against open-source speech codecs on LibriTTS, LJSpeech, and LibriSpeech, matching or exceeding baselines on perceptual quality and speaker simi-larity. 

# 2 Related Work 

Neural speech and audio coding follows an encoder-quantiser-decoder template. WaveNet [82] motivated faster alternatives displacing Opus [81] and LPCNet [80]; SoundStream [97], Lyra V2 [96], EnCodec [16], and DAC [42] established RVQ as the dominant bottleneck. Adversarial and diffusion vocoders-HiFi-GAN [38], MelGAN [41], Parallel WaveGAN [92], UnivNet [30], BigVGAN [49], Vo-cos [72], DiffWave [39]-showed that multi-scale spectrogram losses drive fidelity gains later adopted by codec pipelines [31, 93]. Variants target quantiser structure (HiFi-Codec [93], SuperCodec [105], SNAC [73]), benchmarking [89], semantic alignment [101], single-codebook compression [31], real-time dialogue [17], and dynamic frame rates [51]. Ultra-low-rate work includes DualCodec [50] (12.5 Hz dual-stream), SemantiCodec [53] (diffusion decoder, sub-kbps), TAAE [63] (FSQ, 400-700 bps), TaDiCodec [87] (6.25 Hz, text-guided diffusion), TS3-Codec [90] (pure transformer), X-Codec [95], and NaturalSpeech 3 [32] (factorised VQ). The VQ bottleneck traces to Gray [26] and VQ-VAE [83, 66], with extensions for collapse [19], ro-bustness [44], residual composition [48, 54], inter-codebook optimisation [104], regularisation [106], variable bitrates [9], and FSQ [57], which quantizes each latent coordinate on a fixed scalar grid instead of a learned codebook-all via straight-through gradients [5]; our bottleneck requires no codebook and no straight-through estimator for discretization, as scalar quantization is applied only post-training to the continuous fitted parameters [55]. Codec tokens underpin language models [6, 85, 12, 1, 15, 40, 7, 47, 20, 68] built on transformers [84], self-supervised encoders [60, 3, 28, 14, 11, 65, 4], and multimodal architectures [100]; token qual-2ity directly bounds downstream fidelity [43]. Cross-codec benchmarks [89, 94, 18] and metrics PESQ [67], UTMOS [69], STOI [75] anchor evaluation. Our bottleneck is a parametric signal decomposition rather than a quantiser, following the differen-tiable DSP tradition [23, 27] but applied inside the bottleneck. Gaussian splatting [34, 58] has been extended to 2-D geometry [29], image compression [102, 52, 103, 99, 13], and 1-D time-series [86]; broader radial-basis kernels [64] suggest natural extensions. Feed-forward amortisation [74, 10, 88], implicit compression [22, 21, 77], and Instant-NGP [59] inform our inner-loop design. SIRENs [71] ground per-signal fitting; Lanzendörfer and Wattenhofer [45] apply this to audio by fitting a SIREN per clip-we share that spirit but replace the network with an analytic Gaussian decomposition, making parameters directly quantisable. Rate control contemporaries FlexiCodec [51] and Wav-Tokenizer [31] rely on fixed codebooks; GS-Codec instead varies the resolution of a parametric splat fit. Our SEANet [76] backbone uses the Snake activation [107] and AdamW [56], with a VAE/GAN [37, 25] training skeleton. 

# 3 Method 

We propose GS-Codec, a neural audio compression method whose bottleneck is a parametric signal decomposition based on one-dimensional Gaussian splatting. An encoder network, denoted as 

E and based on the SEANet architecture [97], maps the input waveform to a continuous latent representation. An inner optimization loop then fits this latent as a weighted sum of one-dimensional Gaussian primitives. A SEANet-based decoder network, denoted as D, then reconstructs the audio from this parametric approximation. The entire model is trained end-to-end, with E learning to yield splattable representations and D learning to invert the rendered sum. The overall architecture is depicted in Figure 1. 

Figure 1: GS-Codec pipeline. Training (top): The encoder latent Z is approximated by NG Gaussian primitives via an inner optimization loop. Inference (bottom): A GS Predictor Net regresses primitive parameters in a single pass, which are then quantized, transmitted, and decoded. 

3.1 End-to-End Training with the GS Bottleneck 

The training pipeline consists of the encoder E, the Gaussian-splatting bottleneck, and the decoder 

D, optimized jointly via a combination of reconstruction and adversarial losses. 33.1.1 Architecture Encoder The encoder network E maps a time-domain input waveform x ∈ R1×L, where L is the number of audio samples, to a continuous latent tensor Z ∈ RC×T , where C is the channel dimension and T is the number of temporal latent frames. The network E follows the SEANet convolutional architecture [97] with the Snake activation [107], progressively downsampling the input waveform through strided convolutions to a lower-rate latent representation. Concrete values for C and other architectural hyperparameters are given in Appendix A.

Inspired by 3D Gaussian splatting for scene reconstruction [34], we adapt the formulation to one-dimensional latent fitting. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first application of Gaussian splatting as a compression bottleneck in a neural audio codec. Decoder The decoder network D mirrors the encoder architecture, applying transposed convo-lutions to progressively upsample the reconstructed latent tensor ˆZ ∈ RC×T back to the original 4sample rate. 3.1.2 Loss Functions 

The model is trained using a combination of objectives applied to both the intermediate latent space and the final synthesized waveform. To stabilize training and ensure the encoder latents remain suitable for splatting, we apply a commitment loss inside the bottleneck, defined as Lcommit =

∥Z − sg( ˆZ)∥22, where sg( ·) denotes the stop-gradient operation. This pulls the encoder output toward the splatted reconstruction it has to match. Outer gradients from L reach the encoder E via a straight-through estimator: the decoder receives ˆZSTE = Z + sg( ˆZ − Z), which has the value of the splat ˆZ but carries the gradient of the encoder output Z.The synthesized waveform ˆx is supervised using an outer reconstruction loss comprising an L1 time-domain term LL1 , a multi-scale spectrogram term Lmsspec , an adversarial loss Ladv , and a feature-matching loss Lfeat from a multi-scale Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) discriminator. The total training loss combines all objectives: 

L = λ1LL1 + λsLmsspec + λaLadv + λf Lfeat + λcLcommit , (3) where λ1, λ s, λ a, λ f , λ c ∈ R+ are scalar weighting hyperparameters (concrete values in Appendix A). The gradients from L are backpropagated through D and E via a gradient balancer. 

3.2 Inference 

During inference, the model compresses audio segments into a set of quantized parameters, which are then transmitted and decoded to reconstruct the waveform. 

3.2.1 Parameter Quantization and Token Format 

To achieve compression, each stored parameter of the fitted Gaussians is scalar-quantized to B bits for transmission, where B is the bit depth. The quantization levels are determined via k-means clustering on a held-out calibration subset after training is complete: for each parameter type (scales σi and amplitudes wi,c separately), we fit 2B centroids to the empirical distribution of full-precision values collected from the calibration set. At inference time, each parameter is mapped to its nearest centroid index for transmission, and the receiver looks up the centroid value to dequantize. Positions are handled in one of two ways. In the frozen-position variant, the centers lie on a uniform temporal grid, μi = ( i − 1)( T − 1) /(NG − 1) for i ∈ { 1, . . . , N G} and NG > 1; each μi is a fixed scalar that does not vary with the temporal index t, and no positions are transmitted. In the learned-position variant, positions are optimized per segment and transmitted as additional parameters. Section 4.2.3 compares the two. With frozen positions, the transmitted parameters per segment consist of NG scale values σi and NG × C amplitude values wi,c , each quantized to B bits, giving a theoretical bitrate of 

BR kbps = (C + 1) NGBTseg × 1000 , (4) where Tseg is the duration of the audio segment in seconds. Both NG and B are integers, so the supported rates are discrete. 3.2.2 GS Predictor Net 

The inner optimization loop described above requires K gradient steps per segment at encoding time, which is substantially slower than a feed-forward codebook lookup. To address this, we train a GS Predictor Net P that amortises the inner loop, regressing the Gaussian parameters from the continuous latent Z in a single forward pass. 5Architecture. The predictor takes the encoder latent Z ∈ RC×T as input and outputs the full parameter set: NG scale values σi, the NG × C amplitude matrix wi,c , and NG position values μi.A stack of dilated 1D convolutional blocks first extracts temporal features from Z. A transformer decoder then cross-attends from NG learned queries, one per primitive into these features, producing a per-primitive representation. Three linear output heads map each query to its scale (Softplus), amplitude vector, and position (Sigmoid scaled to [0 , T −1] ). The predictor runs with predicted or frozen centers; in the frozen case the position head is bypassed. Its queries can also be generated from a shared learned token and positional encodings of an NG-point grid, so one checkpoint serves different NG.

Training. The predictor is trained after the main encoder-decoder training is complete, with the encoder and decoder weights frozen. The objective is a simple MSE loss between the rendered splat produced from the predicted parameters and the encoder latent: 

Lpred = ∥ ˆZsplat − Z∥22, (5) where ˆZsplat is obtained by evaluating the rendered approximation with the predicted parameters. The position head uses a higher learning rate than the remaining parameters to accelerate convergence of the temporal placement. 

Inference. At inference time, the predictor replaces the inner loop entirely: the encoder produces 

Z, the predictor outputs the primitive parameters in a single pass, and these are scalar-quantized and transmitted. The decoder side is unchanged. This amortisation reduces the encoding cost from K

optimization steps to a single forward pass through the predictor, while the reconstructed waveform quality remains close to that of the full iterative fit. 

# 4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental setup Datasets. For the main comparison with WavTokenizer-protocol baselines (Table 1), we train GS-Codec on Common Voice [2] ( ≈1,717 h), LibriTTS [98] ( ≈554 h), and VCTK [91] ( ≈68 h), all resampled to 24 kHz monaural ( ≈2,339 h total). Speech reconstruction is evaluated on LibriTTS 

test-clean (clean conditions), LibriTTS test-other (noisy conditions), and LJSpeech (out-of-domain generalisation). For Table 2, we train on the 54,000-hour Librilight-Large dataset [33], resampled to 16 kHz. We evaluate on LibriSpeech test-clean [62]. For the ablation studies (§4.2.2), all models are trained on the same 2,339-hour mixture and evaluated on LibriSpeech test-clean .

Baselines. For Table 1 we compare against EnCodec [16], DAC [42], SpeechTokenizer [101], HiFi-Codec [93], Vocos [72], and WavTokenizer [31], following the evaluation protocol of Ji et al. [31]. Baseline numbers are quoted directly from Ji et al. [31]. For Table 2 we additionally compare against Mimi [17] and XYTokenizer [24]. 

Metrics. We report UTMOS [69] as the primary perceptual quality metric, alongside PESQ [67], STOI [75], and V/UV F1. Wideband PESQ (WB) is used for the WavTokenizer comparison (Table 1); narrowband PESQ (NB) for Table 2. For Table 2 we additionally report VALL-E speaker similarity (SIM) [85] and WER, computed from transcriptions by facebook/hubert-large-ls960-ft [28] on the 4-10 s subset ( n=1 ,237 ). 

Hyperparameters. The SEANet encoder/decoder uses C=32 latent channels, 64 base filters, and Snake activations [107], producing 225 latent frames per 3-second segment at 75 Hz. We train with Adam ( β1=0 .5, β2=0 .9, LR 10 −4) and EMA (decay 0.99 ) for 200 epochs of 2,000 steps on a single NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 GPU ( ≈144 h). The GS bottleneck uses NG=80 primitives per 3-second segment and K=100 inner Adam steps during training. At evaluation time we run 300 steps under a cosine schedule. Positions are fixed to a uniform grid; parameters are scalar-quantised with per-type 

k-means codebooks ( 2B centroids) calibrated on libritts-dev-clean . We report three decode-time variants for each (NG, B ) configuration: unfrozen - positions are optimised per-utterance and transmitted at a fixed Bμ=10 bits, yielding a slight bitrate increase; frozen - positions are fixed to a uniform grid and not transmitted (default); and Pred - the GS Predictor Network (§3.2.2) replaces 6iterative optimisation at encoding time. The two iterative variants serve as upper bounds; Pred is the deployable single-pass codec.

4.2.3 Ablations 

We ablate the design choice of fixing primitive positions to a uniform temporal grid versus allowing them to be optimized freely during the inner loop. This choice represents a critical bitrate optimization, as the additional bits required to transmit learned positions do not translate to large perceptual gains at sufficient primitive counts. Figure 5 reports absolute quality and the metric delta between the two conditions across all swept values of NG at fixed 6-bit post-training quantization.

# 5 Conclusion 

We introduced GS-Codec, a neural audio codec that replaces conventional quantization bottlenecks with a parametric one-dimensional Gaussian-splatting decomposition. Instead of relying on residual vector quantization or finite scalar quantization, GS-Codec represents encoder latents as a weighted sum of Gaussian primitives, enabling compression through structured signal decomposition. Experimental results demonstrate that GS-Codec achieves competitive or superior perceptual quality, intelligibility, and speaker similarity compared to established open-source codecs across multiple bitrate regimes. In addition, the proposed GS Predictor Net amortizes the iterative fitting process into a single forward pass, substantially reducing encoding time while maintaining reconstruction quality close to the full optimization-based approach. 9Figure 4: Bit-depth sweep for NG=60 (orange squares) and NG=80 (blue circles) on LibriSpeech

17 A Training and evaluation details 

SEANet architecture at 24 kHz. The encoder and decoder use the SEANet convolutional archi-tecture [97] with latent dimension C=32 , nfilters =64 base channels, Snake activation [107], strided ratios [8 , 5, 4, 2] (total downsampling factor 320), kernel size 7, residual kernel size 3, 1 residual layer per block, dilation base 2, 2 LSTM layers, weight-norm, and non-causal (bidirectional) convolutions. This yields a 75 Hz latent frame rate and T =225 frames per 3-second segment.

Dataset and training schedule. 200 epochs × 2,000 gradient steps; 500,000 samples drawn per epoch with replacement, batch size 16, 8 data workers. Segments of 3 seconds; shorter clips accepted if ≥ 0.5× segment duration. Trained on a single NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 GPU ( ≈144 hours).

GS Predictor Net training. The predictor is trained with the codec encoder and decoder frozen, using only the splat-reconstruction penalty loss ( λpenalty =1 .0; all other losses disabled). Architecture: 

dmodel =128 , 4 attention heads, 4 transformer blocks, cross-attention from NG learned queries into dilated-conv features of Z. Training: Adam, LR 5×10 −4, cosine schedule (1,000-step warmup, min ratio 0.1), batch size 16, 50 epochs × 2,000 steps, 3-second segments, no refinement iterations (Krefine =0 ), no parameter noise. The predictor is initialised from the codec checkpoint and trained on the same data mixture.

# D Human listening evaluation 

Ten LibriSpeech test-clean utterances (4.0–5.5 s), one per speaker, selected by a seeded random procedure without inspecting method scores. MOS: each condition rated independently on a 1–5 scale (“Listen to each clip once or twice, then rate its overall quality from 1 to 5”). MUSHRA: blinded 0–100 interface with a hidden reference and a 3.5-kHz anchor, randomized order (“Rate the overall audio quality of each sample relative to the reference”). Participants confirmed headphone use and a quiet room; they were unpaid volunteers and took part anonymously. Ratings are averaged within listener, then across listeners; intervals are participant-level bootstrap. MOS gap is natural speech minus system, paired within listener; lower is better. # F Limitations 

GS-Codec has the following limitations. First, encoding is slower than feed-forward codecs: even with the GS Predictor Net, encoding time remains higher than EnCodec and DAC, and the iterative inner loop is substantially slower when the predictor is not used. Second, at lower bitrates (Table 2, 

<3 kbps band), perceptual quality leaves room for improvement; the baselines in this band operate at different rates. Our experiments evaluate English speech at moderate bitrates. Generalisation to other audio domains and sub-kbps coding remains future work. The current codec processes complete 3-second segments with non-causal components, so GPU execution time does not represent streaming latency. 21 G Broader impact 

GS-Codec can reduce the storage and transmission costs of speech. As with other speech codecs, reconstructed audio can retain speaker identity and private information; use should respect data consent and licenses. Downstream speech-synthesis applications share the potential misuse risks of existing audio representations. 22
