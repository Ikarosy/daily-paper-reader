# From Identification to Discovery: Holistic RF Fingerprinting via Learning-Aided Quantization 

Omer Hazan, Gil Paryanti, and Nir Shlezinger 

Abstract —Radio frequency fingerprint identification (RFFI) enables device identification and authentication by exploiting transmitter-specific hardware impairments embedded in received RF signals. Existing RFFI methods are typically designed for a specific operating regime, such as closed-set identification, open-set recognition (OSR), or discovery of new devices, and often treat these tasks as separate algorithmic components. In this paper, we propose a holistic RFFI framework that jointly supports closed-set identification, OSR, and novel class discovery (NCD) within a single expandable architecture. The proposed method formulates RFFI as vector quantization in a learned latent feature space, where registered devices are represented by labeled codewords. This representation enables closed-set identification through codeword assignment, OSR through uncertainty measures induced by the codebook geometry, and NCD by using the latent representations and open-set uncertainty of rejected samples to organize them into new device identities. We further develop a multi-stage training procedure that combines supervised representation learning, latent-space refinement, codebook initialization, and vector-quantization-aware optimization. Experimental results on LoRa and WiFi RFFI datasets demonstrate that the proposed framework achieves accurate identification of registered devices, reliable detection of unseen trans-mitters, and effective discovery and registration of new device classes, highlighting the benefits of coupling the different RFFI tasks. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Wireless devices are increasingly deployed in dense, heterogeneous, and dynamic environments, ranging from Internet-of-Things networks and industrial monitoring systems to critical communication and sensing infrastructures. This growing reliance on wireless connectivity creates a need for reliable mechanisms for identifying and authenticating transmitters based on their emitted signals [2]. Conventional device identification is typically carried out by decoding the transmitted packet and extracting identifiers embedded in protocol headers. While effective in controlled settings, this approach requires access to the decoded content and assumes compatibility with the employed communica-tion protocol. These requirements may be difficult to satisfy in the presence of encryption, proprietary or heterogeneous protocols, limited receiver resources, or non-cooperative transmissions [3]. Radio frequency fingerprint identification (RFFI) provides an alternative identification paradigm that operates directly on the received physical-layer signal [4]. Rather than relying on explicit identifiers encoded in the packet payload or headers, RFFI exploits device-dependent patterns that are inherently embedded

These patterns arise from unavoidable hardware imperfections in the transmitter chain, such as oscillator offsets, in-phase/quadrature (IQ) imbalance, phase noise, nonlinearities, and leakage effects. Since these impairments depend on manufacturing variations and circuit-level properties, they induce RF signatures that are characteristic of individual transmitters [7]. Consequently, RFFI enables passive and non-cooperative device identification, authentication, and monitoring directly from the observed RF signal [8]. The capabilities required from an RFFI system depend on the relation between the devices observed during deployment and those available during system design. This gives rise to three complementary tasks. The first is closed-set identification ,where every received signal is assumed to originate from one of the registered devices observed during training. In this case, RFFI reduces to a supervised classification problem over a fixed device set. The second task is open-set recognition (OSR) , which relaxes this assumption by requiring the system to recognize when an incoming signal is inconsistent with all registered devices and should therefore be rejected as originating from an unknown transmitter. The third task is novel class discovery (NCD) , where signals attributed to unknown transmitters are further organized into previously unseen device identities. When these capabilities are considered jointly, the resulting setting is often referred to as generalized category discovery (GCD) or open-world recognition [9]: the system must identify registered devices, detect signals from novel devices, and incorporate the discovered devices into its identification model. Early RFFI methods relied primarily on manually designed signal descriptors. These approaches exploit the observation that transmitter-specific hardware impairments manifest themselves in measurable RF attributes, such as IQ offset [10], phase-related features [11], IQ imbalance [12], and error vector magnitude [13]. Such handcrafted features are typically provided to classical machine learning classifiers, yielding interpretable identification mechanisms that are directly tied to physical transmitter impairments. Related statistic-based methods instead characterize devices using global properties of the received signal, such as sample moments and other distributional statistics [14]. While these methods highlight the physical origin of RF fingerprints, their performance depends strongly on the chosen features and on the ability of these features to remain discriminative under changing channels, protocols, and acquisition conditions. In recent years, deep learning (DL)-based RFFI has become the dominant paradigm. Deep neural networks (DNNs), mostly convolutional neural networks (CNNs) [15], [16] or recurrent ar-chitectures [17], have been applied directly to raw IQ samples or arXiv:2610.05979v1 [eess.SP] 5 Oct 2026 to time-frequency representations, enabling automatic extraction of discriminative RF features. These methods have substantially improved closed-set identification accuracy, especially when sufficient labeled data are available for the devices of interest. Beyond closed-set classification, DL representations have also been used for few-shot, incremental, and open-set variants of specific emitter identification and RFFI [18]–[25]. Nevertheless, many of these approaches are designed around a particular oper-ating regime, such as identifying a fixed set of devices, detecting unknown devices, or adapting to a small number of new classes. Several recent works have explicitly considered OSR and discovery-oriented RFFI using latent representations learned by neural networks. For OSR, self-supervised or contrastive representation learning has been combined with distance-based anomaly detection in the latent space [16], [26]. Other approaches use probabilistic or prototype-based modeling, including Gaussian prototypes and Mahalanobis-distance-based detection [21], multi-task prototype learning [27], and siamese comparison for open-set category prediction [28]. Additional mechanisms include confidence thresholding with feature enhancement [29] and generative adversarial learning for synthesizing outlier-like signals [22], [30]. For NCD and related discovery settings, latent features extracted by neural networks are commonly clustered to organize samples from previously unseen devices into new classes [21], [31], [32]. To date, existing RFFI pipelines usually treat closed-set identification, OSR, and NCD as separate algorithmic com-ponents. A representation is first learned for classification, a rejection rule is then introduced to detect unknown devices, and clustering or model update procedures are subsequently applied to the rejected samples. This separation leads to two main limitations: (i) methods optimized for closed-set identification do not necessarily provide a representation that can be expanded naturally once new device classes are discovered. Incorporating a new class may therefore require retraining, modifying the classifier, or introducing an additional post-processing mechanism; (ii ) information accumulated in one task is rarely exploited by the next. In particular, OSR often produces uncertainty or deviation measures that reflect how an incoming signal differs from the registered-device distribution, yet these measures are typically used only for binary rejection and are not leveraged to guide the discovery of new device identities. In this paper, we propose a holistic RFFI framework that treats closed-set identification, OSR, and NCD as coupled components of a single open-world identification system. The key idea is to replace the conventional view of RFFI as classification over a fixed set of devices with a representation-based formulation in which devices are described by a learned and expandable set of codewords. The codeword structure supports closed-set identification of registered devices, provides a mechanism for registering newly discovered devices by expanding the codebook, and induces uncertainty measures for detecting signals that do not conform to the registered-device set. The latter is not treated merely as a binary rejection decision; rather, it is used to guide the subsequent NCD stage, thereby coupling detection of unknown devices with their organization into novel device identities. The proposed framework is inspired by recent developments in DL-aided task-based vector quantization [33], [34]. Specifically, we formulate RFFI as vector quantization in a learned latent feature space. A neural feature extractor maps the received signal and informative handcrafted features (HCFs) into a latent representation, which is then associated with a learned codebook. Registered devices are represented by labeled codewords. OSR is performed by evaluating how well the latent representation conforms to the geometry and density structure induced by the registered-device codebook. Samples identified as unknown are then processed by the NCD module, which uses both their latent representations and their open-set uncertainty information to dis-cover new device classes and augment the codebook accordingly. Our main contributions are summarized as follows:  

> •

We introduce a holistic formulation of open-world RFFI that jointly accounts for closed-set identification, OSR, and NCD. Unlike conventional pipelines that treat these tasks as separate stages, the proposed formulation explicitly transfers information from one task to the next, with particular emphasis on using OSR-derived uncertainty to guide novel class discovery.  

> •

We develop a concrete RFFI algorithm based on learned latent representations and codebook-based decision rules. The algorithm combines feature extraction, closed-set identification, open-set scoring, uncertainty-aware buffering of rejected samples, and NCD into a single operational pipeline. The codebook is updated as new device classes are discovered, allowing the identification system to expand without redesigning the overall architecture.  

> •

We propose a detailed multi-stage training procedure for learning the feature extractor and the initial codebook. The training pipeline combines supervised learning, representation refinement, codebook initialization, and vector-quantization-aware optimization, yielding latent fea-tures that are both discriminative for registered devices and suitable for subsequent open-set recognition and discovery.  

> •

We provide an extensive experimental study on LoRa [16] and WiFi [35] RFFI datasets. The experiments systemati-cally evaluate closed-set identification, OSR, and NCD, and demonstrate the benefit of the proposed holistic design. In particular, the results show that the learned vector-quantized representation supports accurate identification of registered devices, reliable detection of unseen transmitters, and effective discovery and incorporation of new device classes. A. Signal Model 

RFFI aims to identify and authenticate wireless transmitters by exploiting device-specific characteristics that are embedded in their emitted RF signals. These characteristics originate from inevitable hardware imperfections in the analog transmit chain, such as oscillator offsets, IQ imbalance, nonlinearities, and leakage effects. Since such impairments depend on manufacturing variations and circuit-level properties, they induce transmitter-dependent distortions that can serve as RF fingerprints. To formulate RFFI, consider a set of wireless transmitters, where the signal emitted by transmitter i is affected by a device-specific transformation. Let s(t) denote the intended baseband signal at time t, and let Fi(·) represent the transformation induced by the hardware chain of transmitter i. The received signal associated with transmitter i is modeled as 

y(t; i) = h(t) ∗ Fi(s(t)) + n(t) (1) where h(t) is the impulse response of the wireless propagation channel, n(t) denotes additive noise, and ∗ is the convolution operator. Accordingly, the mapping Fi(·) captures the transmitter-dependent RF fingerprint, while h(t) and n(t)

represent effects that are not unique to the device and may vary across time, locations, or acquisition conditions.

C. Preliminaries 

Our proposed holistic methodology detailed in Section III is based on tools in vector quantization and its combination with DL. We thus briefly review these necessary preliminaries. 

1) Vector Quantization: The proposed methodology relies on data-driven vector quantization. A quantizer with input dimension d is defined by a finite codebook Q ⊂ Rd, such that each input vector x ∈ Rd is mapped into a B-bit representation 

Q(x) ∈ Q , where B = log 2 |Q| . This mapping is typically performed by selecting the nearest codeword in Q [36, Ch. 23]. When d > 1, this process is referred to as vector quantization, in contrast to scalar quantization, where d = 1 . Rate-distortion theory shows that, under appropriate assumptions, vector quantization can achieve lower distortion than scalar quantization for a given bit budget [36]. A classical method for learning the codebook Q from data is the Linde–Buzo–Gray (LBG) algorithm, in which the codewords are iteratively updated as centroids of clustered data points [37].

H OLISTIC RF F INGERPRINTING METHOD 

The proposed method is designed to realize RFFI as an adaptive open-world identification system, rather than as a collection of separate modules for closed-set classification, OSR, and NCD. To enable such coupling, we formulate RFFI through a learned vector-quantized latent representation . The received signal is mapped into a discriminative feature space, in which registered devices are represented by labeled codewords. This representation is compact, informative, and expandable: closed-set identification is carried out by assigning a sample to the device associated with its closest codeword; OSR is performed by evaluating the relation between the sample and the codebook geometry; and newly discovered devices can be incorporated by augmenting the codebook, without redesigning the feature extractor or retraining the entire identification architecture. The holistic aspect of the proposed framework follows from the way information flows between the different RFFI tasks. The codebook learned for registered devices defines the closed-set identification rule and induces uncertainty measures for open-set recognition. Thus, the proposed method, whose operation and training procedure are detailed in Subsections III-A-III-B, respectively, couples OSR and NCD within a single adaptive RFFI pipeline, where the output of one task informs the next.

B. Training Algorithm 

Our training procedure separates the learning of a reusable RF representation from the registration of the devices encountered in a particular deployment. It therefore consists of two phases. First, we pre-train the feature extractor to produce a device-discriminative latent representation that is also compatible with vector quantization. Then, during enrollment , the feature extractor is frozen and the codebook and OSR model are fitted to the actual registered-device set. This separation is particularly useful in the open-world setting considered here, since changing the registered set does not require retraining the feature extractor. To distinguish these phases, let Dpre denote the labeled dataset used for representation pretraining, with corresponding device set Ipre , and let Den denote the labeled enrollment data associated with the registered set I. The two datasets may correspond to the same devices, but this is not required. 

1) Representation Pretraining: Pretraining is carried out in four successive stages. The stages gradually transition from learning a discriminative continuous representation to optimizing this representation for its eventual vector-quantized use. 

Stage 1 (Contrastive representation learning): We first train the feature extractor fθ (·) using supervised contrastive learning [44]. For each training signal, two augmented views are generated using RF-domain transformations, including random phase rotation, circular time shifting, additive noise, and amplitude scaling. The latter captures variations in received signal strength due to the propagation channel. A temporary projection head pξ(·) is applied to the resulting latent vectors, and the standard supervised contrastive objective is minimized such that representations originating from the same transmitter are attracted while those associated with different transmitters are separated. The projection head is discarded after this stage. Consequently, the output of Stage 1 is a feature extractor whose continuous latent space exhibits compact device-specific regions and separation between different devices.

C. Discussion 

Holistic Open-World RFFI: Our framework is tailored to open-world RFFI by coupling the identification tasks through a shared and expandable representation. Casting closed-set identification as vector quantization allows registered devices to be represented by codewords, with newly discovered devices incorporated by augmenting the codebook. The enrollment procedure further decouples representation learning from the registered-device population, enabling the same latent representation to be reused as the device set evolves. Beyond this structural coupling, our approach transfers information between tasks: the continuous OSR score is retained after the rejection decision and exploited by OSRWC to guide NCD, assigning greater influence to samples that more strongly deviate from the registered-device distribution. Finally, the density-based formulation is well suited to open-world operation, since the OSR model is fitted using registered-device data, while HDBSCAN does not require prior knowledge of the number of unseen devices and can leave insufficiently supported samples unassigned. Our framework thus addresses the key challenges motivating our holistic formulation: it supports expansion of the registered-device set and exploits information produced by one RFFI task to improve the subsequent task. 

Computational Complexity: The computational cost is dominated by the neural feature extractor. For an input frame of 256 IQ samples (as in Section IV), the complete pipeline requires approximately 180 .5 MFLOPs per frame, of which more than 99 .9% are associated with the convolutional feature extractor. The HCF branch, feature fusion, codeword comparison, and density-based OSR introduce only a small additional cost. In particular, increasing the codebook size from P = 32 to 

P = 256 increases the complexity only from 180 .46 MFLOPs to 180 .51 MFLOPs per frame. Thus, expanding the registered devices through codebook augmentation has negligible impact on online inference complexity and does not require additional CNN inference or retraining. The more involved HDBSCAN and OSRWC operations are executed only when a discovery is triggered and are thus outside the continuous per-frame processing path; in the evaluated WiSig configuration, OSRWC incurs approximately 87 .4 MFLOPs per discovery episode. 

Possible Extensions: The proposed design admits several natural extensions. The vector-quantized representation can incorporate alternative codebook-learning and quantization-aware training strategies, while the density-based OSR stage can be generalized to alternative distance or density measures better suited to particular channel conditions. Likewise, the proposed transfer of OSR information to NCD through OSRWC represents one mechanism for coupling the two tasks; future work may consider clustering methods that incorporate the continuous open-set confidence directly. Finally, continual adaptation of the feature extractor could complement the present codebook-level expansion when long-term changes in propagation conditions or device characteristics substantially alter the learned latent representation. IV. E XPERIMENTAL STUDY 

We empirically evaluate our RFFI framework on two RF datasets 1. Our study is designed to assess whetter: (i) the learned vector-quantized representation preserves accurate identification of registered devices; (ii ) our density-based OSR mechanism reliably detects unseen transmitters; (iii ) transferring the OSR in-formation to the discovery stage improves NCD; and (iv ) newly discovered devices can be incorporated while preserving identifi-cation and open-set capabilities. We additionally evaluate robust-ness to signal degradation and isolate the contribution of the pro-posed cross-task information transfer through an ablation study.

A. Experimental Setup 1) Datasets: We distinguish between the data used for representation pretraining and those used for enrollment. Let Ipre

Samples used for discovery are separated from those used to evaluate the system after registration. 

LoRa: We use the LoRa RFFI dataset [16], following the enrollment and rogue-device evaluation protocol of [45] where applicable. This setting evaluates the ability to reuse a representation learned from one transmitter population for a different registered-device population. For each enrolled device, packets 0–99 are used for enrollment, while packets 100 –299 are retained for closed-set and OSR evaluation. For each enrolled transmitter, 70% of the available packets are used for codebook construction, quantization-aware enrollment, and fitting the registered-device density model, while the remaining 30% are retained for evaluation. The unseen transmitters are similarly divided between samples used for OSR/discovery and held-out samples used after registration.
